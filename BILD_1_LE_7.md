## Activation Energy

### Diamond to Graphite

- The conversion of diamond to graphite (pencil lead) has a negative ΔG.
- Is this reaction spontaneous?
	- A. Yes
	- B. No
- Then how it is that we can put diamonds into rings and expect them to stay there?

#### Key Point
- Spontaneous doesn't always mean it happens quickly!

### Activation Energy Concept

- The initial energy need to get a chemical reaction going is called the activation energy (EA).

![[activationEnergyDiagram.png]]

### Energy Requirements

- You need to put in energy to get a reaction started
	- For example, if you want to set something on fire, you first have to hold a match to it.
	- Chemically, you have to first break the bonds in a molecule to allow the reaction to happen.
- Some reactions need more energy to get started than others.
	- Gasoline: Small spark will light it on fire
	- Damp wood: Need a lot of effort to set it on fire

### Comparing Activation Energies

- Which reaction has a higher activation energy?
	- A. Conversion of diamond into graphite
	- B. Ice melting at room temperature

### Size of Activation Energy

- The lower the activation energy, the more likelier it is to proceed, so overall the reaction will happen more quickly.

![[reactionProgress.png]]

### Energy Sources

- Generally, heat from the environment
	- Heat makes molecules move randomly, which can give them enough energy to break bonds (overcome the activation energy)
	- More heat = more molecular movement
	- Less heat = less molecular movement

### Problem in Biology

- Most reactions in your body have a high activation energy, which you're not going overcome very often at body temperature.

### Enzyme Effects on Reactions

- Can enzymes change a nonspontaneous reaction into a spontaneous one?
	- A. Yes
	- B. No

![[enzymeReactionGraph.png]]

#### Answer
- No: Enzymes do not affect ΔG
- ΔG just has to do with the energies of the products and reactants, and enzymes are not products or reactants

### Activation Energy Independence

- Activation energy is independent of ΔG
	- Spontaneous, but with a high EA
	- Not spontaneous, but with a low EA

## Structure of Enzymes

- Enzymes are specific for catalyzing a particular reaction
- They have an active site where reactants bind
	- Vocabulary: The reactants that bind to the enzyme are called the enzyme's substrates
	- Enzyme names often have something to do with their substrate and end with -ase

![[enzymeStructure.png]]

### Active Site Function

- Active site: Where the substrates bind

### Enzyme-Substrate Interaction

1. Active site is available for substrate molecules
2. Substrates enter active site
3. They induce a change in the shape of the enzyme
4. Substrates react and turn into products
5. Products are released

![[enzymeSubstrateCycle.png]]

### Shape Changes

- Enzymes change shape when they bind to substrates to facilitate reactions

![[enzymeShapeChange.png]]

### Active Site Mechanism

#### How the active site can lower the activation energy:
- Orienting substrates correctly
	- Easier to form bonds between the substrates
- Straining substrate bonds
	- Easier to break the old bonds
- Providing a favorable microenvironment
	- Example: provide an acidic or basic environment to encourage the addition or subtraction of hydrogen atoms
- Covalently bonding to the substrate
	- Makes it easier for another reaction to occur
	- Enzyme structure is always restored afterwards

### Example: Enolase

Features of the enolase active site:
- 2 side chains protrude into active site

#### Key Components

### Active Site Modifications

#### Question: Would the reaction go if the active site lysine were replaced with aspartate?

![[lysineAspartateComparison.png]]

- A. Yes
- B. No
- C. Can't tell

**Answer: B. No**
- Aspartate has a negative charge on the side chain, so it can't replace lysine's positive charge.

#### Question: Would the primary structure of the enzyme change if the active site lysine were replaced with arginine?

![[lysineArginineComparison.png]]

- A. Yes
- B. No
- C. Can't tell

**Answer: A. Yes**
- Because the amino acid sequence is changed

#### Question: Would the reaction go if the active site lysine were replaced with arginine?

- A. Probably yes
- B. Probably no
- C. Really can't tell

**Answer: A. Probably yes**
- It has the important chemistry (the +)
- But, the Arg one might not work as well
- You'd have to try and see

#### Question: Will the tertiary structure definitely change if the active site lysine were replaced with arginine?

- A. Probably yes
- B. Probably no
- C. Really can't tell

**Answer: B. Probably no**
- That lysine is interacting with the substrate, not holding the tertiary structure together

### Summary: Enzyme Changes at the Active Site

- Many side chains at the enzyme active sites have particular functions to interact with substrates.
	- Chemical changes (like a mutation that switches one amino acid for another) that disrupt that function will almost certainly mess up the ability of the enzyme to facilitate the reaction.
	- Chemical changes that don't disrupt that function may slow down a reaction, but the reaction will probably continue to some extent.

### Environmental Effects on Enzymes

#### Temperature Effects
- The protein ovalbumin typically exists around 22-37°C
- What might happen to it if it is heated to 70°C?

**Result:**
- It changes its shape and loses its function!
	- Non-covalent bonds break, and the protein loses its secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structure
	- Technical term is denature
- Enzymes generally have the highest activity in conditions that they evolved in

### PCR and COVID Testing

In COVID tests, we first make lots of copies of the viral genome as DNA to make it easier to detect. To copy DNA in the lab, you need to heat it to 94°C to get the strands to separate and then use an enzyme called a polymerase to do the copying. What kind of polymerase should you use?

A. Human polymerase
B. Viral polymerase
C. Polymerase from an E. coli human gut bacterium
D. Polymerase from Yellowstone National Park hot spring bacterium

**Answer: D. Polymerase from Yellowstone National Park hot spring bacterium**
- Human or E. coli polymerase will denature at 94°C!
- This Taq polymerase is crucial for PCR, a fundamental technology for COVID tests, biotech, and anything to do with DNA
