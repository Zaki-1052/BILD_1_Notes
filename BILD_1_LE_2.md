# BILD 1 Lecture 2
- ## 4/2
## Matter
### Overview
- **Identify different types of matter based on whether they are atoms, elements, molecules, or compounds**.
- Organisms are composed of **matter**
	- Matter is anything that takes up space and has mass
- Matter consists of chemical **elements** in pure form and in combinations called **compounds**
	- each element consists of unique **atoms**
### Atoms
- The smallest unit of matter that retains properties of an element
- Has three main parts (protons, neutrons, **electrons**).
- The basic building blocks of matter.
- Examples: hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen
- The **number of protons** (+) determines an atom's **identity**. 
	- Oxygen has 8 protons.
- An atom's **electron** (e) **distribution** determines its ability to form bonds.
	- Oxygen has space for 2 more electrons, so it can form 2 bonds.
### Elements
- Elements consist of **one type of atom** and can be identified by their atomic numbers 
	- (number of protons)
- **Elements** cannot be broken down to other substances by chemical reactions
- Example: oxygen’s atomic number is 8
- Four elements make up 96% of living matter
	- Why is oxygen the most common element found in the human body?
		- cellular respiration
		- metabolism (nutrients)
		- oxygen transport (hemoglobin)
		- cellular processes
### Molecules and compounds
- **Molecules**: Formed when **two or more atoms** join together by covalent bonds.
	- Can be the same atoms.
		- Ex: N2 H2 O2
- **Compounds**: Found when two **different elements** join together.
- A compound is made of atoms joined by bonds. 
	- Formic acid (CH202) consists of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O).
		- CO2 HO2
- Atoms and bonds govern how molecules in a cell work together. 
	- If molecules don’t interact properly, it can lead to disease.
- Wood ants (*Formica rufa*) use chemistry to ward off enemies
	- formic acid
- A compound has characteristics (emergent properties) different from those of its elements
	- Na (sodium) + Cl (chlorine gas) = NaCl (sodium chloride)
### Electrons and potential energy
- **Energy** is the capacity to cause change
- **Potential energy** is the energy that matter possesses because of its location or structure
- Matter has a natural tendency to move toward the lowest possible state of potential energy
- The electrons of an atom differ in their amounts of potential energy based on their distance from the nucleus
![[electronShells.png]]
- Electron distribution diagrams for the first 18 elements in the periodic table
- **Valence electrons** are those in the outermost shell, or **valence shell**
- The chemical behavior of an atom is mostly determined by the number of valence electrons
![[electronDistribution.png]]
- Valence = bonding capacity 
	- (usually the number of unpaired electrons)
- What is the valence of the following elements?
	- Hydrogen: 1
	- Oxygen: 2
	- Nitrogen: 3
	- Carbon: 4
- The formation and function of molecules and ionic compounds depend on **chemical bonding** between atoms
- Atoms with incomplete valence shells can **share or transfer** valence electrons with certain other atoms
- These interactions usually result in atoms staying close together, held by attractions called **chemical bonds**
## Bonds
- Distinguish between a **covalent bond** and an **ionic bond** in terms of the energy needed to break it and what types of atoms can engage in the bond.
### Covalent bonds
- A **covalent bond** is the **sharing** of a pair of valence electrons by two atoms
- In a covalent bond, the shared electrons count as part of each atom’s valence shell
- A single covalent bond, or **single bond**, is the sharing of one pair of valence electrons
- A double covalent bond, or **double bond**, is the sharing of two pairs of valence electrons
- A triple covalent bond, or **triple bond**, is the sharing of three pairs of valence electrons
- A **molecule** consists of two or more **atoms** held together by **covalent bonds**
#### Covalent bond notation
- The notation used to represent atoms and bonding is called a structural formula
	- For example, H—H represents a single bond
	- O═O represents a double bond
- This can be abbreviated further with a molecular formula
	- For example, H2
- Remember:
	- *Covalent bonds can form between atoms of the **same element or atoms of different elements*** 
![[covalentBonding.png]]
![[covalentBonds.png]]
### Electronegativity
- Electronegativity is the capability of an atom to **attract electrons** to itself. 
- An atom with high electronegativity has a stronger electron attraction than an atom with low electronegativity
- For example, when two hydrogen atoms bond together, they share their electrons equally because they have the same electronegativity
- Polar vs non-polar bonds
	- Dictated by differences in electronegativity
- Examples:
	- Carbon = 2.5
	- Hydrogen = 2.1
	- O = 3.5
- *The difference in electronegativities determines whether a covalent bond is polar or nonpolar*

| Type of bond       | Difference in electronegativity |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Non-polar covalent | < 0.5                           |
| Polar covalent     | Between 0.5 and 2.1             |
- There are two types of covalent bonds, **nonpolar** and **polar**. 
- **Nonpolar covalent bond**
	- occurs when two atoms share **electrons equally** because the **electronegativity** of both atoms is **relatively equal**
- **Polar covalent bond**
	- occurs when **one atom is more electronegative**, and the atoms **do not share the electron equally**
		- The atom that is **more** electronegative has a **stronger attraction** for electrons, so electrons spend more time in the electron cloud of that atom.
		- Unequal sharing of electrons causes a partial positive or negative charge for each atom or molecule
		- The atom with the higher electronegativity will gain a partial negative charge.
- When an oxygen atom is bonded to a hydrogen atom ( O-H ), what will the electric charge be on the oxygen atom?
	- A. No charge
	- **B. Partial negative charge**
	- C. Partial positive charge
	- Choose all the atoms that have partial **positive** charges:
		- CH4
		- **NH3**
		- **H2O**
### Ionic bonds
- An **ionic bond** is a chemical bond that involves the **transfer** of electrons from one atom to another so that both atoms will be left with a full valence shell
	- The two resulting oppositely charged atoms or molecules are called ions

| Type of bond       | Difference in electronegativity |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Non-polar covalent | < 0.5                           |
| Polar covalent     | Between 0.5 and 2.1             |
| Ionic bond         | \> 2.1                          |
- **Ionic bond example**: 
	- between sodium and chlorine atoms
- A sodium atom has one electron in its valence shell
- A chlorine atom has seven electrons in its valence shell
- The sodium atom will donate its one valence electron to the chlorine atom so both of them will have completed valence shells
![[sodiumChloride.png]]
- **Compounds formed by ionic bonds are called salts**
- Salts, such as sodium chloride (NaCl; table salt), are often found in nature as crystals
- Most salts are quite stable when dry, but dissociate quite easily in water
- Sodium (Na+) ions are used to help regulate blood volume and blood pressure
- Chloride (Cl-) ions can be used to help balance the pH of the body and to transmit nerve impulses
- What is likely to happen to the electrons when a potassium atom bonds with a chlorine atom?
	1. Chlorine loses an electron and potassium gains an electron.
	2. Potassium and chlorine share electrons.
	3. ***Potassium loses an electron and chlorine gains an electron.***
	4. None of the above
- Which of the following molecule(s) contains a **nonpolar covalent bond**?
	1. NaCl
	2. ***N2***
	3. MgO
	4. ***C2H2***
- Which of the following molecules contains a polar covalent bond(s)?
	1. Li2O
	2. NaCl
	3. ***H2O***
	4. NaF
### Hydrogen bonds
- A **hydrogen bond** forms when a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to one electronegative atom is also **attracted** to another electronegative atom
- In living cells, the electronegative partners are usually oxygen or nitrogen atoms
#### Hydrogen bonds require: 
1. A hydrogen with a **partial positive charge** due to it being covalently bonded to a much more electronegative atom, such as O or N .
2. A highly electronegative terminal atom with a **partial negative charge** due to it being covalently bonded to a much less electronegative atom, using $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{O}$ and $\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{F}$ as examples.
3. Both hydrogen-bonding functional groups must be within approximately an atomic diameter for the hydrogen bond to form.
![[hydrogenBond.png]]
- **Example**: 
	- **Hydrogen bonds hold both strands of the DNA double-helix together.**
- Hydrogen bonds can easily be broken and reformed like unzipping and zipping a zipper in order for DNA to be replicated or transcribed
- Stronger phosphodiester bonds in the DNA backbone hold each strand together
![[doubleHelix.png.png]]
### Bonds
- Covalent (electrons are shared)
- Non-polar (shared equally)
- Polar (shared unequally)
- Ionic (electrons are transferred)
- Hydrogen (no electrons are shared or transferred)
